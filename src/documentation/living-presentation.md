# The living presentation — a plan to review

**Status: final plan, 2026-10-30. The flagship presentation. Building has started with the first
layer: the hypercomb tile, its six values, and the opening page.** This page gathers the whole plan for an interactive presentation of
how Hypercomb works. It is written to be listened to: plain sentences, no
tables, and every file reference kept in the last section so it can be
skipped when read aloud.

What this revision settles. The presentation opens on hypercomb, then the
primitive, a word, its signature, a layer, and a meta layer, and then builds
the cube from that primitive on screen in seven moves, before anything else.
A tile's current layer is its heart. Where a tile came from is a trait called
its origin, and lineage keeps its meaning, a tile's path from the root and its
history. A layout reaches down until a child defines its own, and
then collapses to its own layer along that branch. The story is a grammar:
a tile's children must read as language. Part discovery is a critical part of the design:
asking for parts by their words with and, or, and not, across every domain
you follow, answered as one flat list of offers. The presentation is about
Hypercomb, and its job
is to welcome people into this world in a visual and interactive way.
Because every piece follows the doctrine and depends on nothing, the
arrangement can be rebuilt later without breaking anything, so it does not
have to be perfect before it starts. The first layer is the values layer
under one tile called hypercomb, and the presentation shows how that single layer is
built, in detail, before anything else: values as words, the layer read flat
as the presentation, every step in as a slice by signed name, layouts and
parts meeting only at a trait word, your own layer stack of traits at every
level, and the choice of what fills each one. There is one cube for each domain of each
participant, served from that participant's own hosts. A participant builds
their cube by replicating other people's packages and creating their own.
What they have published is the public cube, and the root of their domain is
where that public cube is shown. Every publicly deployed item is a place in
that cube. Your workbench is hypercomb.io, or your own host's root domain: private to
your machine, and able to share publicly once your host is registered for
publishing. Trial subdomains are the isolated, public debugging
environments. Natural
language is both the scripting language and the debugging language. The cube
draws three axes, but nothing limits it to three. And the cubes join into one
tapestry through the signatures they share.

## What this is, and the one point that matters most

This is Hypercomb's flagship presentation. It follows its own values, in how
it is made and in how it is shown, and it grows into an interactive way to
view any grammar along any dimension, with an interactive cube that can carry
as many dimensions as there are traits.

### It follows its own values

- **Simple.** It starts from one primitive and one act, and grows one layer
  at a time, never more.
- **Organized.** Every step is its own build revision, nothing is ever
  deleted, and any number of organizations can be laid over the same pieces.
- **Collaborative.** It is published where anyone can open it, every piece
  can be copied and changed, and each domain grows its own variations.
- **Intelligent.** People and AI use the same words under the same rules,
  automatons answer the grammar, and every change waits for a person.
- **Accessible.** It is written in plain words that can be listened to, it
  works on a phone, it respects reduced motion, and reading it needs no
  account.
- **Localized.** Every word can be translated by the community, and locale
  is one of the cube's own dimensions.

### Where it ends up: a grammar viewer

The cube built in the opening does not stay a picture of this presentation.
It becomes a tool. Point it at any tile, and it shows that tile's grammar: its
words, their versions, where they came from, and any other trait, as
directions you can turn and choose. The presentation is the first grammar it
shows. After that, it shows anyone's.

In a sense it becomes a tesseract. With a fourth trait, the cube becomes the
cube of four dimensions, drawn the way a tesseract is usually drawn: a cube
inside a cube, with every point in the inner cube joined to its twin in the
outer one. The fourth trait runs along those joins. Turning the tesseract
through its fourth direction is choosing which traits are drawn, so the points
flow between the inner and outer cubes as the view turns. Every further trait
is one more dimension of the same shape, a hypercube, which is close to what
the name Hypercomb already says.

This is a welcome. It is about Hypercomb, and it invites people into this
world in a way they can see and touch: tiles that lift, open, and answer when
you click them. It is not a slide deck in the usual sense. It is an interactive diagram
you can move through in more than one direction, and every piece of it lives
in the hive.

The most important point the presentation has to make is this: **the
hexagons are the program.** Every tile in the hive is an automaton. It is
where the code and the content live. Anyone can copy it, change it, and make
it their own, and the original stays exactly as it was. The presentation
proves this by being made of the same tiles it describes.

That is why it is a living document. It is never finished. Anyone can open
any piece of it, read how it works, copy it, improve it, and publish their
version. The people who follow it get a small notice, and they can take the
new version, take just one piece of it, or leave it.

### Where the story is going

The story builds toward one picture: a cube of points.

Every point is a tile, one automaton at one version. Each participant has a
cube for each domain they own, served from their own hosts. They fill it two
ways: by replicating packages from the people they follow, and by creating
packages of their own.

The points a participant has published make up their public cube. That is
the cube everyone else sees of their domain, and the cube everyone debugs
against. The root of each public domain is where its public cube is shown,
and every publicly deployed item has its own place in that cube.

Turning a cube shows the same points divided different ways: by the word of
the language each point answers, by version, and by origin, where it came
from. Those are the three directions a screen can draw, but every trait a
tile wears is another direction, and there can be as many as anyone wants.


Finally, the cubes are connected. Wherever two cubes hold the same point, a
thread joins them. Starting from the root and running outward, those threads
weave every participant's cube into one tapestry of connected domains. Every
cube has the same shape and is read the same way, and a signature means the
same thing in every cube that holds it. That symmetry is what lets people
share in each other's work, and it is what the presentation ends on.

The cube comes first. It is built on screen from the primitive in the
opening, and everything after it opens one point of the cube and shows what
it is, where it lives, and how it got there.

## The opening: one primitive, built into the cube

### The story is a grammar

The story is built one layer at a time, one axiom at a time, and it builds a
grammar as it goes. The children of any tile should read as language: put
them together and they make sense as words under that word, and the path from
hypercomb to any tile should read aloud as a phrase. Each layer has one
relation to the tile above it. If a tile's children cannot be put together as
language, the naming is off, and the fix is the names.

### The parts of speech, in Hypercomb

If tile names are nouns, the grammar has to say what every other kind of
word is. Each part of speech already has a place in the hive:

- **Nouns are tiles.** A noun is something you can stand in and point at. Its
  word is its address. Tile names are nouns.
- **Verbs are behaviour words.** A verb is something you say to make an act
  happen: create, trait, fill, publish, take. Every act has a verb, and a verb
  is never a tile name, so a sentence never has to guess which one is meant.
- **Adjectives are marks.** An adjective describes; it never holds. It is a
  mark a tile wears: a value, a trait, a tag. When an adjective is also a
  tile, as the values are, that tile is the gathering of everything wearing
  its mark.
- **Connectives are the small words inside a sentence:** on, as, into, from,
  at, and the logic words and, or, and not. They join the other words and are
  never tiles or behaviours of their own.
- **Processes are verbs, not tiles.** A word ending in "ing" names an act in
  progress, so it belongs to a behaviour. The tile for that idea is its noun:
  the act is publish, and the tile is publications.

And each layer says its relation once, in its meta layer, and the relation
decides the part of speech of its children. A layer whose relation is "is"
holds adjectives: hypercomb is simple, organized, collaborative, intelligent,
accessible, and localized. A layer whose relation is "has" or "is made of"
holds nouns: the primitive is made of a word, a signature, a layer, and a
meta layer. So the path from hypercomb to any tile reads aloud, and every
word in it is the kind of word its place says it should be.

### Hypercomb first, then the primitive

The presentation opens on one hexagon, named hypercomb. At this point it is
only a label.

Then the hexagon opens, and shows what it is made of. This is the primitive,
and there is only one: a **word**, its **signature**, a **layer** of children
under it, and a **meta** layer that says what it means. No new object is
introduced. Hypercomb is the first primitive, and nothing in the story will be
a different kind of thing.

Everything after this is the primitive used again, with only the words
changing. A server is a tile with a word, a signature, a layer of files, and
a meta layer. So is a domain, a package, a revision, a participant, a cube, a
point in the cube, a word of a sentence, and a theme. The presentation's own
pieces follow the same rule: each stage, each slide, each scene, and the
transition bee are tiles, so the presentation has no private structure of its
own. If a plane ever turns up that cannot be described as word, signature,
layer, and meta, that is a hole in the primitive, and the fix is the
primitive, never a second kind of thing.

The primitive also has exactly one act: take a word, get its signature, get
its heart, the current layer, and read its children. A person clicking and an
agent speaking do the same thing, and the presentation shows that one act on
every plane, each time with different words:

- on the values layer, take simple, and read what wears its mark;
- in a server, take a name, and follow it to the root signature and its files;
- in a layout, take a trait word, and read which part answers it;
- in the cube, take a word, an origin, and a version, and read the point;
- in a sentence, take each word in turn, and the path is the run.

### From one hexagon to the cube, in seven moves

The cube is not saved for later. It is built first, from the primitive, on
screen, so the viewer watches the primitive become the cube, and every later
stage is just opening one point of something they have already seen built.
Each part of the primitive gives a direction, so no axis has to be invented.
Each move ends on one sentence, said plainly.

**Move one, one hexagon.** Hypercomb sits alone, then opens into its four
parts: word, signature, layer, and meta. It ends: this is hypercomb, and for
now it is only a name.

**Move two, the layer gives breadth.** Under the tile, its layer lays out as
a row: the six values, simple, organized, collaborative, intelligent,
accessible, and localized, side by side. Each one opens to the same four
parts, so the same shape, made six more times, proves the shape. This row is
the word direction: a row of words under one parent. It ends: a layer is the
words that sit under one word.

**Move three, rows under rows.** Each value opens into a row of its own, and
there are rows under rows. A tile's layer is made of tiles, and each of those
has a layer. The plane becomes a stack of planes. It ends: every word can
hold a layer of its own.

**Move four, the signature gives depth.** One tile changes, and gets a new
signature. The old one does not vanish. It stays, and the new one sits above
it. Every tile does the same, and the stack becomes a column. The top of each
column is the tile's heart: its current layer, the one that answers now.
Moving the heart is what makes the heartbeat. The heart needs no new
functionality. It is the newest marker in the tile's
history, which is already what every read lands on, what a publish points to,
and what an update notice compares. Every change adds a new marker on top,
and the heart simply moves up to it.
Everything beneath the heart is its history. This is the version direction,
and it shows that nothing is ever deleted without having to say so. This is
also where the one honest caveat is said, once: versions are signatures for
each tile, not shared numbered layers, so revision two in one place and
revision two in another are different things, lined up only so the eye can
compare them. It ends: nothing is replaced; every change is kept, one above
the other.

**Move five, origin gives width.** The same word can be answered by a tile
from somewhere else: your own, or one replicated from someone you follow.
Where a tile came from is a trait it wears, like any other mark, and it is
called its origin. Three words stay distinct: lineage is the tile's path from
the root and its history, the heart is its current layer, and the origin is
where it came from. Tiles with different origins sit side by side at the same
word and version, and that is the third direction. An origin can be a chain: a
tile replicated from someone who replicated it from someone else carries that
whole path, drawn as a thread running back through other cubes. That is the
tapestry appearing early, before it is named. It ends: the same word can come
from many hands.

**Move six, the cube appears.** Every tile is now a point, and the points
fill a cube. No cube was drawn. The primitive was repeated in three
directions, and the cube is what that looks like. It ends: we never drew a
cube; this is the primitive, repeated.

**Move seven, meta and traits give every other direction.** One point opens
its meta layer. Whatever it says about the point is another word that can
divide the points, the same way as the first three. Platform, locale,
audience, and reviews are traits exactly as origin is, and origin was only
the first one shown. A trait picker lets the viewer choose any three traits
to draw as the cube's directions. So nothing limits the cube to three, and
that is no longer a promise: it follows from the structure. Add a fourth
trait and the cube becomes a tesseract, a cube inside a cube, and the points
flow between the two as it turns. The opening ends
on the one sentence the viewer should keep: everything here is the same
thing, used again.

### The primitive marker and the same-shape check

Two devices keep the primitive in view for the whole presentation.

**The primitive marker.** A small hexagon is pinned in the corner of every
scene, with its four parts lit. It pulses as each direction of the cube is
added, so the viewer connects each part of the primitive to the direction it
made. Later, whenever the viewer clicks anything, the marker pulses and the
clicked object snaps open into the same four parts. Over the course of the
story it becomes the signature of the presentation.

**The same-shape check.** On any plane, one control lays the outline of the
primitive over whatever is on screen: a server, a domain, a cube, a word. It
fits every time. That is the proof, in action, of the main point: the
hexagons are the program.

### After the cube, every stage enters it

Once the cube is built, the stages are ways of entering it. Each one zooms
into a place the viewer has already seen built. Stage one opens a server and
shows that it is a point. Stage two opens a domain. Stage three shows the
other participants, each with a cube of their own. The stage that used to
build the cube now returns to it, for the public cube, its faces, and slices.

### Rendered with the hive's own behaviours

The presentation has no private renderer. Everything it shows is added to the
render as a behaviour of the hive, the same kind of thing as the slides,
website, and tree views a tile can already turn on. The cube, the primitive
marker, the same-shape check, the trait picker, and the lift of hexagons into
pages are each a behaviour, so any tile can use them, not only the
presentation's. The heart, the origin, and every other trait are read by the
render the way it reads any mark. And anyone can render the presentation with
their own behaviours instead, through their own layer stack, the same way
they would choose any other filler.

There is one thing to guard against: recursion. The presentation is made of
the tiles it shows, so a behaviour can end up showing a tile that shows that
same behaviour: the cube drawing the tile that holds the cube, or the
presentation drawn inside itself. The guard is simple because everything has
a signature. Each render carries the signatures already open along its path.
When it meets one of them again, it draws that tile closed, as a hexagon you
can click into, and never opens it again in place. The hive already walks its
groups of related tiles this way, keeping track of where it has been so that
a cycle cannot spin.

## The first layer: one layer, built in detail

Right after the cube is built, the presentation slows down and shows how a
single layer works, in full: the first layer, the one the cube's first row
came from. This is the part that explains how everything in Hypercomb is
organized and how it works. Every later layer works the same way.

### The hypercomb tile: a label, or a filter

The first tile is called hypercomb. Here its name is only a label, the name
the hive uses for itself. But the same tile could just as easily be a filter
over the graph: a tile that shows whatever is gathered under a word. In the
hive, a question like that is a tile of its own. The scene shows both
readings of the one tile, so the viewer sees that a tile can be a name or a
question.

### The values layer

Under hypercomb is one layer: the values. Simple, organized, collaborative,
intelligent, accessible, and localized. These are the same words as the
original title of hypercomb.io. Each value is a tile, and its word is its
address.

A value needs no definition to work. Its meaning grows with whatever is
related to it. A piece, such as a document, a behaviour, or a feature, relates
to a value by wearing a mark, and it can relate to several values at once.
That is how generic words keep all their meanings: a word that means several
things simply gathers several kinds of pieces.

Where a value does need its meaning said, it is described in the value's meta
layer: the small layer that describes a tile rather than holding its content.
The description is versioned like everything else, so it can be changed or
replaced without touching the value or anything related to it. The same holds
for every tile at every level: if it needs meaning, its meta layer says it.

### Read flat, the layer is the presentation

Read the values layer as a list and you have the presentation's first view.
There is no separate presentation to keep in step with the hive. The slides
are this layer, seen flat. The stages that follow are one saved walk through
the structure: next follows the walk, explore leaves it.

### Every step in is a slice by signed name

Whether a person clicks or an agent speaks, every step into a layer is the
same act. A name is taken. Its signature gives an address. The address gives
the current version of the layer. That version names its children. The scene
draws the walk as a chain of signed names, and shows the slice that comes back
at the end of it. It is the same walk for a person and for an agent, and the
same slice for anyone who asks with the same names.

### Extrude parts when they are needed, not before

Every piece starts as one thing: one tile with one page that carries its own
look and works on its own. Any piece that could have a different
presentation or visual can be given web parts, but nobody plans them in
advance. When a piece actually needs a second look, it is broken apart then,
and the extruding happens at the layout layer. The layout gains one more
trait word, a new entrance point, and the part that needs its own look
becomes the tile named that word, plugged in at that entrance. The piece
itself is not cut up by hand; the layout simply names one more word, and the
part answers to it.

What answers is a new primitive atom: the smallest piece that stands on its
own, with one job and its own look, that has that trait. Because it answers to
a word rather than to a particular layout, the same atom can fill that word
in any layout that asks for it, and other people can offer their own atoms
for the same word, side by side. So every extrusion adds to a shared stock of
primitive atoms, each named by the traits it has, and the next layout that
needs one of those words finds it already there.

A trait can also be a tag. Both are words, and a word has one address, so a
tag and a trait spelled the same are the same place. Tagging an atom with a
word lists it where a layout asking for that trait will look, and the traits
an atom has are words you can search by, like any tag. One word does both
jobs: it says what a layout wants, and it says what an atom is for.

This is how data that belongs to several things gets its meaning. An atom
can carry as many traits and tags as it has uses, and each word places it
somewhere it belongs, without copying it and without any of those places
owning it. The same atom can be a header in one layout, an example under a
value, and a tagged item in a search, all at once, and it means something in
each of them because of the word that put it there.

Until a piece needs a second look, it stays whole, and it is already
finished. Because nothing depends on anything, extruding later never breaks
what was there.

### Layout and parts meet only at a word

Take a website. It needs a layout, and it needs parts. The layout does not
hold the parts. It declares traits: the words it leaves open, such as header,
hero, menu, and footer. A trait is a word interface. A part knows nothing
about the layout. It only answers to a word, by being an immediate child of
the target tile named that word, or by wearing a fill mark for it.

So the layout stays separate from the parts, and it reflects back which parts
of the application are visible. A filled trait shows its part. A trait that
nothing fills yet shows as a hexagon silhouette, the outline of a tile that is
not there yet. At publish time, what is visible is sealed into one signed tree.
Because every part sits as a child of its target, named by its word, all of a
layer's web artifacts are stored in one predictable place, and any layer can
take parts gracefully.

### A layout reaches down until a child defines its own

A layout is defined at one layer, and it reaches down through the layers
beneath it. A layout can override its parent's. When a child layer defines
its own layout, the parent layout stops reaching below the layer it was
defined on. It keeps governing its own layer, and below that the child's
layout takes over.

Nothing is deleted or rewritten. The parent layout keeps its signature and
stays exactly as it was; only its reach changes, and since the reach follows a
choice, it undoes like any other choice. A layer's look is always explained by
one layout, the nearest one defined at or above it, and that layout is
complete by itself: it never half-inherits from above. And it fits the fixed
order for filling a trait, where your own choice comes first: defining a
layout on the child layer is that choice, so it wins.

The collapse happens along that branch only. The parent keeps reaching down
every other branch that has not defined its own, because one child's choice
must never change what a sibling runs. And the whole parent layout collapses,
not just the one trait that was overridden, so every layout stays complete.

In the scene, the layout is drawn as a shaded region running down through the
layers. When a child defines its own, the parent's shading retracts upward to
its own layer along that branch, and the child's shading fills in below. The
viewer sees the collapse happen.

### The layout behaviour: the extrude names decide what shows

The layout is a behaviour a layer turns on, and what it holds is a list of
extrude names: the trait words. The extrusions are already there. They are
the tiles that are this layer's children, each named by its word, so a layout
adds nothing new. It only says which of those names this layer shows.

Change the names and the layer changes with them. Leave a name out and the
child of that name is blocked out: it is still there, untouched, it just is
not shown. Add a name and one more extruded trait is allowed, and the child
of that name, or a silhouette waiting for it, appears. Nothing is deleted
either way, so a name put back brings its child straight back.

A different layout is not a new structure. It is a new version of the
current layer, recorded in that layer's own history like any other change.
Going back to an earlier layout is going back in that history, and every
layout the layer has had stays there to return to.

### The grammar: markers first, then tiles extrude from them

Traits are markers, so working with layouts needs words, and the words come
first. Place the markers, and the tiles extrude out from them.

A trait is a tile that has not been set yet. It renders natively in the hive
as a placeholder: a hexagon silhouette, named by its word, sitting exactly
where its tile will land, so it reminds you it is there. The placeholder
carries an extrude icon. Press it and the placeholder extrudes into a real
tile of that name, which is the same act as saying create with the word. Leave
it, and it stays a placeholder, which is a finished state, not a missing
piece. Almost all of
the grammar exists already, in the trait word:

- **trait** says the traits on the tile you stand in, and how each is filled.
- **trait**, followed by words, places those markers. Each one shows as a
  placeholder with its extrude icon. Today the word also extrudes every tile
  at once; in this design the tiles extrude when the icon is pressed, or when
  create is said with the word.
- **trait own**, followed by words, places markers the tile already fills
  itself, so no tiles are made.
- **trait ~**, followed by a word, takes a marker away. Its tile stays.
- **fill**, followed by a word, chooses what fills a marker when there is
  more than one candidate.

Only one new word is needed, for the layout behaviour itself:

- **layout** says whether the layer shows only its markers' tiles, and which
  children are left out.
- **layout on** makes the layer show only the tiles its markers extruded, in
  the markers' order, and leave every other child out.
- **layout off** shows every child again.

So the order of markers is the layout, placing a marker is allowing one more
extruded trait, and taking one away blocks it out. The new names are few:
a **marker** is a trait placed on a layer, an **extrusion** is the tile a
marker makes, and a child **left out** is one no marker names while the
layout is on.

The first layer is planted this way: the hypercomb tile is made, and then its
six values are placed on it as markers, so the six value tiles extrude out of
it.

### Sharing themes, styles, and code: no dependencies

This has to be a perfect way to share design themes, styles, and code, and
everybody must follow the same pattern: no dependencies.

**The rule.** No item depends on another item. The one thing an item may
depend on is an interface: a word that belongs to neither side. The test is
one question: am I naming a shape, or naming a thing? A word is a shape, and
anything that fits it will do. A particular file is a thing, and it would
have to exist, be that one, and be in one place. So an item names words,
never things.

**Share by signature, connect by word.** Every theme, style sheet, font,
image, layout, and code file is stored once, as a file named by the signature
of its own bytes. Everyone who uses it shares that one file. But no item
names that file. An item names only the words it needs: the theme words it
styles itself through, such as ink, paper, accent, and type; the traits it
leaves open; the names of the modules it calls. Which signature answers each
word is recorded once, in the layer data, as a fill. The items stay free, the
file stays shared, and the choice lives in one place.

**Every item stands alone.** An item carries its own working defaults, so it
looks and works correctly with nothing filled at all. A theme only changes
what the theme words resolve to. Remove the theme and every item still
renders, in its own defaults. That is what makes an empty slot a finished
state rather than a missing piece.

**Themes are a layer of style references that parts pull.** A theme is
itself a layer. Its children are named by the theme words, ink, paper,
accent, type, and each one holds a reference to a style by signature. A part
does not receive a theme. When it renders, it pulls the style for each word it
uses from whichever theme layer is chosen, and falls back to its own default
for any word the theme leaves out. Choosing a theme for a layer is one fill in
its layer data. Switching themes changes that one fill, and nothing inside any
part changes. One theme can dress any number of layers, and any number of
themes can be offered for the same words, side by side, from anyone's host.

Themes stack like every other layer. Your own theme layer can name only the
few words you want to change, and every other word is pulled from the theme
beneath it. A style for one part works the same way, one size smaller: it is a
filler for that part's words, never a sheet one part reaches into another to
use.

**A layout is a hierarchy of trait entrance points.** A layout holds no parts
and no markup. It is a tree of words. The top of the layout declares its
traits, such as header, body, and footer. Each trait is the entrance point to
the next layer: the tile named that word. That tile can declare traits of its
own, such as menu and search inside header, and so on down. The whole layout is
the shape of those words, and nothing else. Because each entrance point is
only a word, any part that answers to it can come in, at any depth.

**The HTML lives in the parts.** Each part carries its own markup, its own
appearance, and its own behaviour, in its own tile. The page is assembled
from whichever parts answer the layout's words, and at publish time what is
visible is sealed into one signed tree. No part's markup is written into the
layout, and no part's markup is written into another part.

**Parts talk freely, without knowing each other.** Parts must be able to
communicate, and they do it through words too. A part says something under a
word, such as cart-changed or theme-switched, and any part listening for that
word hears it. The hive's modules already talk this way, through named
effects, and a part that arrives late still hears the last thing said under
the word, so the order parts load in never matters. No part names another
part. Remove the part that speaks and the listener still works, in its own
defaults. Add a second part that speaks the same word and every listener hears
it, with nothing rewired.

**Code.** Modules already follow this. A module may import only from the core.
It reaches other modules through their registered names and the effects they
announce, never by importing them. A shared library is reached by its alias
name, and the hive maps that name to a signature when it loads. A bee never
contains another bee, and the build refuses a bee that tries.

**Versions and upgrades.** A fill holds the exact signature that was chosen,
so nothing changes underneath an item. When a theme, style, or module changes,
it is a new file with a new signature. A layer moves to it by changing one
fill, an incremental update, or a full upgrade moves many fills at once. Where
your own change meets an incoming one, the AI drafts the merge and you accept
it or keep yours. Because the layer data names every fill, publishing a layer
carries what it uses, and replication brings it to every host that serves it.

The scene shows the shared files once, below the layer, and the words each
item needs as small sockets on the item. Threads run from each socket to the
file that fills it, and every thread passes through the layer's fills. Swap
the theme and one fill changes: every thread through it moves at once, and no
item is touched. Then the layout is drawn as its tree of words, each word an
open entrance, and parts drop into the entrances that match them. Finally the
parts start talking: a word lights up as one part says it, and every part
listening for it lights up in turn, with no line drawn between the parts
themselves.

### Your own layer stack: traits added and removed at every level

A tile's traits come in a stack. First the traits its view declares, then
the traits its behaviours declare, then the traits opened on the tile itself.
Anyone can define their own layer in that stack, adding a trait with the
trait word, or forgetting one given there. A creation with a different set of
traits is a different creation, with its own signature, and it can still
reuse every part the old one had.

From any level, the trait word lists what that level can take: every trait,
and how each is filled. That is the list of parts implementable at that
layer. And because each trait word is an address, the same word on the hosts
you follow lists what other people have already built for it. This works the
same way at every level, from the first layer down.

### Debug mode: the parts are automatons

In tiles view, with debug mode on, the parts lift off their hexagons, animate
into their places in the layout, and then turn into their working interface.
The viewer sees that every part of the page is a tile, and that every tile
runs: they are automatons. This is the main job of the transition bee.

### The container that expects the word, and the choice

Last, the scene builds the container: a tile that declares the trait it
expects, for example hero. Then it shows the choice. A trait is filled in a
fixed order. First, your own explicit choice. Then a child named the word.
Then the behaviour's own working version. If the trait is one that must be
provided, the search goes on: tiles named the word elsewhere in your hive,
then the same word on the hosts you follow. If nothing is found anywhere, the
gap stands as an ask, which a person, an agent, or a community host can
answer.

When several candidates are found, they are offered, never picked on their
own. You choose with the fill word. That choice is what makes the program
yours, and changing it later is one more choice, which undoes like any other.

## Part discovery: and, or, not, across domains

This is a critical part of the whole design, so it has its own section, and
the presentation gives it its own scene. It is how anyone finds the parts
that already exist, here and on every domain they follow, before making
anything new.

Because every atom carries its words, the parts can be gathered by them.
Ask for every part that has these words, or those, and leave out the ones
with that one: and, or, and not. "Header and dark" narrows to parts that have
both. "Header or footer" gathers parts that have either. "Not draft" leaves
out anything marked draft. The answer is a flat list of parts, from this
page, from the whole branch, or from the whole hive, and it reads just like
the values layer read flat.

A question like this can itself be kept as a tile. That is the other reading
of the hypercomb tile from the first layer: instead of a label, a
tile can hold a question over the graph and show whatever answers it, kept
current as atoms gain and lose words. It is the hive's one operation, saying
a word and narrowing, written down so it can be asked again.

The same question reaches across domains. A word has one address on every
host, so each host you follow can list what it holds under that word, read
the way any public website is read: a plain fetch, with no joining and no
room to enter. Your hive asks each followed host for the words in your
question, combines the answers with and, or, and not on your own machine, and
shows one flat list of parts from every domain at once, each marked with the
domain it came from. Narrow it with another word, and the list narrows across
all of them.

Nothing in that list runs or moves into your hive by being found. A part from
another domain is an offer. You open it to look, and you take it, whole or
as one piece, only by choosing to, with its code held until you accept it.
And because you only ever ask about words you hold, nothing arrives that you
did not ask for. This is how the cubes of the tapestry are searched: one
question, answered by every domain you follow, each answer still living on
its owner's host.

## Any organization of the assets

Because every asset carries its own words and depends on nothing, the assets
can be organized in any way at all, and in many ways at once. An organization
is just another layout of words, or another saved question, laid over the
same assets. It holds nothing and moves nothing. Make one that groups by
value, one that follows the story, one for a single domain, one for a single
audience; every asset stays exactly where it is, and each organization simply
reads it.

That is what makes reorganizing free. A new organization is made, not
migrated. The old one can stay, beside it, for anyone who still uses it, and
removing one never removes an asset. The structure this presentation starts
with, the hypercomb tile and its values, is only the first organization, and
it can be joined by others, or rebuilt, whenever that helps.

## Every piece of the presentation is a tile

Every element you see on screen is its own tile in the hive. A server is a
tile. A domain is a tile. Each subdomain, each package, each revision, each
cube, each face of a cube, and each word of a sentence is a tile.

Each tile carries its own page. When you click an element in a diagram, you
go into its tile, one level down, and its page opens. That page explains the
element in more detail, and it has its own children that go deeper still. So
the presentation is only the top layer. Underneath it, the same tiles become
a complete technical description of the whole system, one layer at a time.

This works with what the hive already has:

- A tile's page lives in its own website slot, and the path of tiles is the
  route. A link to a child tile moves one level down and opens that tile's
  page.
- A slide is any tile marked as a slide. The presentation itself is a website
  artifact that sits beside its slides, not a box that holds them. The slides
  are joined to it with the enroll word, and the present word plays them.
- An object that appears in several stages, like a server or a package, is
  one tile reused by its signature. Improve its explanation once and every
  slide that uses it shows the improvement.
- A tile with no page yet is still a real place you can stand. So the whole
  tree can exist before every page is written.
- When many pages change in one pass, one build record names them all, so the
  whole presentation can be undone and restored as a single step.

### The same four levels everywhere

Every object follows the same four levels of depth, so anyone diving in knows
what they will find:

1. **The slide.** The object as it appears in the diagram, in a few words.
2. **The object.** What it is and what it does, with its own small diagram.
3. **The mechanism.** The records, formats, and flows behind it. For example
   the signed index, the derivation record, or the install pointer.
4. **The source.** The document and the code it comes from, read by
   signature.

The four levels are four views of the same primitive, so going deeper never
brings in new vocabulary: each level is the same word, signature, layer, and
meta, read more closely. This also answers the question of audience. The slide level is plain
language for anyone. One level down is for people who want to know how it
works. The bottom level is for developers.

## The opening move: hexagons lift into their pages

Every scene opens the same way, and this is the visual that carries the main
point.

First, the stage shows the real hive, with the tiles for this scene as
hexagons on the grid. Then those hexagons rise off the plane into three
dimensions. They tilt toward the viewer and show their depth. While they are
in the air, each hexagon's outline reshapes into a panel, and its page fades
in, right where its tile was. Finally the pages settle into the scene's
layout, already connected by lines where they relate to each other.

When you leave a scene, it plays in reverse. The pages fold back into
hexagons and settle onto the grid. The viewer always sees that a page and a
tile are the same thing.

### How the lift is built

The hive draws its hexagons in two dimensions, with Pixi. Moving the whole
renderer to three.js is only a proposal, and this effect does not need it. A
tile's page is placed directly into the live page, not into a separate frame.
That means a small module, a transition bee, can do the whole effect:

- read where each hexagon sits on screen from the grid;
- place a hexagon-shaped element exactly over it;
- lift it and reshape it with the browser's own three-dimensional transforms;
- hand over to the real page the moment the page arrives.

The transition bee is a module like any other: signed, replaceable, and
itself a tile in the presentation.

### Copy it, change it

Every page that lifts out shows that it is a tile. It shows its signature,
who made it, and two actions: **copy into my hive**, and **change it**. A copy
gets its own signature, and the original is untouched. When you publish your
version, the people who follow you see the update notice described in stage
three, and they decide whether to take it.

A change written by a model never goes live on its own. It waits in the hive
until a person accepts it. A model may put a change on a trial subdomain for
review, but promoting it, taking it, and accepting it are human acts.

## Choosing your way through: the octopus

The presentation branches like a many-armed octopus with arms on every
level. The same rule applies on every page, at every depth.

At the end of every page there are two ways forward. **Explore** takes you
into any object on the page, one level down. **Next** continues the main
story at the same level. When you explore an object, its page ends the same
way, with its own objects to explore and its own next. So every arm has arms
of its own.

Two more moves are always available. **Back** returns you to exactly the page
and view you came from. It never works out a destination from the route; the
hive already behaves this way when you step into a view. **Where am I** folds
the page you are on back into hexagons, so the hive itself becomes the map of
where you are.

These match the three directions the hive's viewers already use. Moving
within a viewer is next. Moving between viewers is explore. Moving across is
stepping sideways to the next object at the same level.

## Domains and subdomains: the foundation

This section is the explanation that stage two's slides, the eight
participants, and the cubes all draw on. It is told here in full, once.

### Your workbench is your root, private to your machine

Your workbench is hypercomb.io, or the root domain of your own host. When you
open it, the hive runs in your browser and keeps everything in your browser's
own storage, on your machine. It is private: nothing you make there leaves
your machine unless you publish it.

It is also the place you publish from. To share your work publicly, you
register your host for publishing. Sharing requires hosting: anyone can
watch and bring things in without a host, but only a registered host can
make your work available to others. Once it is registered, the same
workbench can publish to your domain, open trials, and promote them.

### A domain is yours, served from your own hosts

A participant claims a domain with one sentence: domain claim, followed by the
domain name. The hive answers with two nameservers. The participant sets them
at their registrar, and that change is the proof that they control the
domain. Nothing else is asked.

From then on, nobody else can publish there. The key that claimed the domain
is the key that publishes to it. Each participant's domain is served from
their own hosts, and that domain, with all its names and all its versions, is
one participant's cube. Their subdomains, trials included, are inside their
cube, not cubes of their own.

### The root domain: your workbench, and everyone else's view of your public cube

The bare domain, the root, plays two parts at once. To you, on your own
machine, it is your workbench, with everything you keep there private to your
browser. To everyone else it is your front door, and in this plan it is where
your public cube is shown. Open the root of any public domain from another
machine and you see the cube made of everything that domain has published,
every version of it, from every source. The same address gives two views,
because what a browser stores belongs to that browser alone.

The same holds one level down. **Every publicly deployed item is a place in
that public cube.** A promoted site, a published package, a deployed page:
each has a root signature, and that root signature is its position in the
cube. Open a deployed item from its root and the cube appears centred on it,
with its versions running down beneath it and the pieces it is made of
reaching out to the points they came from.

Today the root of a domain shows its showcase: the tiles of what it
publishes. The public cube is a second way of seeing exactly the same
published points. It needs no new storage, because it is a view of what the
root already holds.

### One folder, many names

A domain has one folder: its root. Every name under the domain is only a way
of reaching that folder. No subdomain owns any files. A subdomain is
resolution, never storage.

The root holds only three kinds of thing. Atoms: the bytes of everything
published, each file named by the hash of its own bytes. Histories: the
ordered markers of each branch, where the highest marker is the current
head. And pools of meaning: the signed indexes, packages, listings, and every
other grouping. The start-up files for the shell sit in the root too, the same
for every name, so any name can be opened from cold.

### Publishing is bytes first, then a pointer

Publishing to a name happens in two steps, always in this order. First, the
bytes go into the root, each file under its own signature. Identical content
is one file, however many names or people point at it. Then the pointer moves:
the publisher's signed index is updated so that name now opens on the new
head.

A new version is a new head. The old heads keep resolving, so older versions
keep working. Taking a name down is the same act in reverse: the pointer is
removed. No file is deleted.

### The trial subdomain is the debugging environment

Every change starts on your workbench, privately. When you want others to
try it, it goes to a trial subdomain. Its name is the word "try", a dash, and
the name of the site it will become. A trial for the cards site
lives at try-cards, on the same domain, and when it is promoted it becomes
the cards site itself.

A trial subdomain is completely isolated:

- **It has its own origin.** The browser gives it its own storage, so nothing
  it does can touch anyone's hive. The origin is the sandbox.
- **It writes nothing.** The host refuses every write that comes from a trial
  address.
- **It holds no keys.** At a trial address the shell stores no AI key, signs
  nothing, and keeps any signing extension out of reach. A bar across the top
  says plainly that you are at a trial.
- **It cannot reach your machine.** Its page policy blocks it from talking to
  anything on the visitor's own computer.
- **It opens in its own tab,** never inside another page.

Inside that bubble, a trial runs readable source that has not been minified.
Anyone can open it, try it, and read exactly what changed, file by file. The
host's AI reads it, people read it, and signed reviews gather beside it.

The what-changed panel always shows where a change stands, in four steps:
draft, trial, reviewed, and your choice. At the last step, anyone can take
the change into their own hive, where it is held until they accept it. Only
the publisher can promote it or withdraw it.

A trial is not part of the public cube. It floats at the edge, in its own
bubble, until it is promoted.

### Promotion is one pointer move

When a trial is ready, promoting it moves one pointer. The site now opens on
exactly the files the trial ran. Nothing is rebuilt and nothing is uploaded
again. From that moment the promoted version is a point in the public cube,
and it stays there for good.

Withdrawing a trial removes its pointer; the trial address then says there is
nothing here, and its files stay in the root under their signatures,
unreachable but never deleted.

### Why everyone's files stay stable

Put these together and every person can work at their own level on files that
do not move under them.

**No version is removed.** Bytes are never deleted. A new version is a new
head, and every old head still resolves. If you stand on revision one, it
keeps working for as long as any host serves it. The public cube only grows.

**Nobody else can change what you run.** Updates are taken on the following
side, only when you have turned updates on, and only when you choose. A
version you hold stays until you upgrade.

**Trials cannot leak.** Each trial is sealed in its own origin and writes
nothing, so a broken draft breaks only its own bubble. Ten people can debug
ten changes to the same package at the same time, and none of them can
disturb the others or the live site.

**Every publisher's path to their running code stays whole.** Follow the
chain for any publisher: their domain, then their signed index, then the
head it names, then every file in that head's tree. Each step is signed or
hashed, and each file is checked against its own name when it arrives. A
host cannot change a byte without being caught. Another publisher's broken
change cannot enter that chain, because it can only arrive by being taken and
then accepted by hand. So every publisher keeps a clean path from their
domain to the code that runs.

### The honest limits

The presentation should state these plainly, one level down from the slides.

- **Control is a signed word, not enforcement.** Withdrawing a name is
  followed by honest hosts and readers, but nothing can make another host
  forget bytes it already holds. Private content is encrypted before it
  leaves the device.
- **Publishing a branch's history is a choice.** The bytes of every published
  version stay in the root, but sharing the full step-by-step history of a
  branch is switched on per branch, because it carries every earlier state.
- **The one mutable file.** The shell's start-up pointer, the pin, is the one
  file a compromised host could swap. Today the loader checks that the bytes
  match the pin and falls back to the last pin that worked. Choosing the pin
  from the publisher's signed pointer, so that a compromised host can cost
  you at most a missing page, is designed but not built.
- **A recycled name must start empty.** Today the hive's trust in code is
  keyed by domain alone. It should be keyed by domain and publisher key
  together, so a name given to someone new inherits nothing. This is a known
  gap.
- **A separate address is not trust in its host.** Whoever runs a host
  decides what it serves. Your own domain on your own host is the only place
  where that someone is you. Several hosts, each serving different things,
  keep a breach at one from reaching the rest.

## Natural language: the scripting language and the debugging language

This section is the explanation that the stage on working in the cube draws on.

### A sentence is the program

In Hypercomb, natural language is the scripting language. A sentence typed on
the command line is the program. Each word is declared by the behaviour that
owns it, with the shape of what it takes. Before a sentence runs, the hive
works out how far it reaches: which tiles it touches, whether it leaves the
machine, and which of its steps would change something. Every step that would
change something waits for a person's approval. A model speaks the same
sentences a person does, under the same rules, and a capability with no word
is treated as a gap in the language to be filled, not hidden behind a
private shortcut.

### The same grammar, run by different automatons

A word means one thing everywhere. The implementation behind it is a mark on
the tile. So one sentence can be answered by different automatons: the same
words, run through a participant's own implementation, or through one they
replicated from someone else, or through an older version they still hold.
This is what makes the cube useful. Changing which point answers a word
changes the program without changing the sentence.

### Debugging is spoken too

Debugging uses the same language. Every step of finding and fixing a problem
is a sentence a person or a model can say, read back, and repeat. These words
already exist:

- **module trials** lists every open trial on a domain: whose it is, what it
  changes, and how the host's AI read it.
- **module changes**, followed by a trial's name, opens that trial file by
  file, before and after, with the reviews beside it.
- **module audit** reads a trial's changed files by signature, from your own
  hive, and compares them with what you run. It runs nothing.
- **module review** asks the host's AI to read the change again.
- **module assess** signs your own reading of a trial: accept, refuse, or
  unclear, with a note.
- **module take** brings a trial's changed layer into your own hive as a pick,
  held until you accept it with brood accept.
- **module drop**, followed by a path, puts your own trunk back at that path.
- **read** and **find** say what a tile holds and where a word appears.

Because every debugging step is a sentence, a debugging session is itself a
record: the sentences you said, and the slice of the cube you said them
against. That record is a set of signatures. Hand it to someone else and they
can replay exactly what you saw.

### What is not built yet

Two pieces of this are proposed, not built. First, a word that steps one
point of a slice up or down its versions, so that halving the range to find
the version that broke something is one sentence at a time. Second, one
sentence calling modules that live in other participants' cubes, on their own
hosts, and bringing the results back. The spoken-programs document still
marks that second piece as a hypothesis.

## The stages

The stages are one saved walk through the structure built in the first
layer: next follows the walk, and explore leaves it at any point. There is no
fixed number of them. The presentation grows over time, and new stages, or
new walks, join it as they are made. The stages share
one picture. A strip of servers runs across the top of the
screen. It starts as a single server in stage one, opens up to show its
domain and subdomains in stage two, splits into eight participants in stage
three, and in stage four the cube built in the opening returns, one for each
participant's domain. The cubes stay for stage five.

### Stage one: one server, one host, one client

**Slide one, the three roles.** These are easy to blur, so they are separated
first. A server is the machine that answers: your desktop app, a provider
like Cloudflare, or a friend's machine. A server is simply a folder of files
answered over the web. A host is a domain you own, which you change through
its host interface. Whoever's machine answers for it, the host is still
yours. A client is the browser running the Hypercomb core, with its own
private storage holding files named by their signatures, the history of each
tile, and pools of meaning. You can hover over each role to see what it
holds.

**Slide two, inside a server.** Open the server and you see files named by
their signatures, the publisher's signed index, which says which version each
name opens on, and install pointers, which name the current package. A server
only shares what the current hive reaches; nothing else leaves. Click a file
and you see that its name is the hash of its own bytes.

**Slide three, one request from start to finish.** A visitor types an
address. The core reads which publisher and which tile that name is bound to,
reads the publisher's signed index, finds the current root signature, and
then fetches each file by its signature. Every file is checked as it arrives.
A tamper button corrupts one file on the server, and the visitor refuses it.
This is why a stranger's server is safe to use: the worst it can do is fail
to answer. It cannot change what you get.

### Stage two: domains, subdomains, and the workbench

**Slide one, your workbench, a domain, and its names.** The stage opens on
the viewer's own workbench: hypercomb.io, or their own host's root, drawn
inside their machine with a lock on it, because everything there is private.
A switch labelled register for publishing connects it to their host. Then
the server opens to show one domain,
using the real Plugin the Matrix publications page with its subdomains for
Revolución, Meetup, Arkanoid, and others. All the names lead to one root
folder. Click a subdomain and you see that it holds no files, only a pointer
in the signed index. Press publish and the viewer watches the two steps: the
bytes drop into the root, then the pointer moves. The old pointer's files are
still there, and the old version still opens. A small cube icon sits on the
root: this is where the public cube will be shown, once the viewer knows what
a cube is.

**Slide two, the trial subdomain.** A trial appears beside the cards site,
drawn inside its own bubble: try-cards. The bubble shows its walls: its own
storage, no writes, no keys, no reach into your machine. The viewer edits one
line of the draft and watches its signature change. Then three more trial
bubbles appear, other people working on the same package at once, and none of
them touches the others or the live site. The four steps of the what-changed
panel run along the bottom: draft, trial, reviewed, your choice. Signed
reviews from the host's AI and from people gather on the bubble. Along the
bottom, the sentences that drive it are shown as they are said: module
commit, module changes, module assess.

**Slide three, promote, and the visitor's exact version.** The publisher says
module promote. The bubble's pointer slides over to the cards site; no file
moves. The visitor's copy then goes through the minify step and comes out
smaller, with its own tree of signatures that still names the readable source
it came from. A slider moves between the readable and minified versions and
shows the real numbers: the package went from about nine megabytes to about
six. A visitor gets that exact version, pinned to its root signature, and it
never changes underneath them. A re-derive button runs the minify step again
on the readable source and arrives at the same root, which is how anyone can
check that the minified code is exactly what it claims to be.

Stage two ends with the lifecycle as a loop: say it, draft on your private
workbench, trial subdomain, review, promote, deployed, visitors pinned, and
back to the next sentence.
This loop becomes a small icon that every participant carries from stage
three on.

### Stage three: eight participants, revisions, and checking in

**Slide one, the split.** The single server rises and splits into eight. On a
phone they form two rows of four. Each one is a participant serving their own
domain from their own hosts. Each shows its root, its live subdomains as solid
lanes, and its trial subdomains as small bubbles at the edge.

Each participant has their own colour, and every colour also has a pattern,
so colour is never the only signal. A filled chip is a package this
participant created. A hatched chip, in another participant's colour, is a
package this participant replicated from someone they follow; it keeps its
original signature and colour. A chip inside a bubble is a trial, not yet
promoted. Stacked rings on a chip are the revisions it holds.

There are six packages: a shared core, cards, journal, calendar, chat, and
theme. Each participant holds two to four of them, so every package sits with
at least three participants, and cards sits with five. That overlap is what
the rest of the story depends on. Click a package and every participant
holding the same signature lights up. Each holds the same bytes, and every
copy is identical and checkable, so it does not matter whose host answers.

**Slide two, someone changes a shared package.** The third participant works
on cards in a trial bubble, then promotes it. The new bytes have a new
signature, so this is revision two. A small notification badge appears on the
participants who follow the third participant and have updates turned on.
Nothing moves on its own; a version you hold stays until you choose.
Participants who do not follow them stay on revision one, and revision one
still works, because nothing is ever deleted. A visitor pinned to revision
one is not affected at all.

At the same time, the sixth participant also starts from revision one, in
their own trial bubble, and makes their own revision two. Both versions exist
side by side, with different signatures and their own histories. Neither
overwrites the other, and anyone can build on either one. A trace line runs
from each participant's domain through their signed index to their running
code, and every line stays whole through all of this. That is the picture of
every publisher keeping a clean path.

**Slide three, find it, then take it whole or take a piece.** It starts with
finding. The viewer types a question over words, such as header and dark, not
draft, and it goes out to every participant's domain at once. Matching parts
light up across all eight, each marked with its owner's colour, and the flat
list of answers gathers at the bottom. Adding a word narrows every domain
together; removing one widens them. Nothing found has moved anywhere yet.

Then the choice. Open a badge, or a part from the list, and you have
three choices. **Upgrade** takes the revision as it is. **Skip** leaves it.
**Take a piece** takes only one layer of it into your own hive, at its own
path, and leaves the rest of your build exactly as it was. The piece is held
until you accept it, and when you commit, it is laid into your build where it
belongs. The viewer drags one layer from revision two into their own build
and watches everything else stay put. Each choice is also shown as the
sentence that does it.

This is what makes so many flavours possible. Everyone can mix the pieces
that suit their own build or machine, plug them in, and pass their mix on.

### Stage four: the cube

Each of the eight participants' domains rises into its own cube, and the view
moves into one of them. **There is one cube per domain per participant.**

**You build your cube by replicating other people's work and creating your
own packages.** Every package you take from someone you follow is replicated
onto your own hosts, under its original signature, and becomes points in
your cube. Every package you make becomes points of your own.

A cube is a cloud of points, and **each point is one tile**: a specific
automaton, one implementation at one version. This is the cube the opening
built from the primitive, and its three directions are the ones the viewer
watched appear. Across is the **origin**, a trait: where the point came from.
The participant's own packages sit at the centre, and the domains they
replicated from spread out on either side, so the cube grows from its own
root outward. Into the page is the **word**: the word of the grammar the
point answers, the layer direction. Down is the **version**, the signature
direction, newest at the top.

Not every position has a point. An empty spot means this domain has no
implementation of that word at that version from that origin. One spot can
also hold more than one point: the two separate revision twos from stage three
sit side by side.

**Slide one, the points and the public cube.** The cube fills in point by
point, each glowing in the colour of the participant it came from. You can
turn the cube freely, and clicking a point opens its tile.

Then the cube divides in two. **The published points are the public cube.**
Everything this participant has published, every version of it, stays in the
lattice. That is the cube everyone else sees of this domain, and it is the
cube the public debugs against. Drafts and trials move out to the edge, each
in its own small bubble, because they are not part of anything that runs
until they are promoted. When one is promoted, it drops into place and stays
there for good. So the public cube only grows, and never shifts under
anyone.

Finally the view pulls back to the participant's root domain, and the cube
icon from stage two opens: the root is where this public cube is shown.
Click any deployed item, a site or a package, and the cube turns to centre on
it, with its versions running down beneath it.

**Slide two, three faces, three views of the same points, and more.** Turning
the cube to a face flattens one axis away, and the points slide into the
groups that face shows. The points never change. Only the way they are
divided changes.

- **The top face** flattens the versions. It shows origins against words:
  what this domain runs right now, and whose work each piece is. Picking a
  row draws a line through one word across every origin.
- **The front face** flattens the words. It shows origins against versions:
  how the participant's own work, and each piece they replicated, has changed
  over time.
- **The side face** flattens the origins. It shows words against versions.
  This is the language view: every implementation of every word in this
  domain, and how it changed, wherever it came from.

The cube shows three axes because a screen can show three at once, not
because Hypercomb stops at three. Nothing prevents adding more. In the hive,
a new dimension is just another mark a tile can wear, and filtering by a mark
is a view, so a new axis costs nothing to keep in step: no copy is made and
nothing has to be synchronised. Some dimensions already exist in the hive as
marks or pools: **platform**, such as web, desktop, or phone; **locale**, the
community's translations of each word; **audience**, which projection of a
domain a given group is shown; and **reviews**, the signed reviews and checks
gathered on each point. These are traits, exactly as origin is. The trait
picker from the opening lets the viewer choose any three to draw as the
cube's directions. The points stay the same. Every
face is still a flattening of one chosen axis, so the same way of reading
works however many dimensions there are.

**Slide three, slices.** A slice is one point chosen for each word. Every
slice whose choices fit together is a working build. A slice is not a flat
plane through the cube. It is a bent surface that dips to different depths
and crosses between origins. You can grab a point and drag the slice up or
down to a different version, or across to another participant's
implementation; dependency lines show whether it still fits.

The cube is drawn from demo data at first. A later step can draw it from the
real history of the package tiles, so that changing a tile in the hive adds a
new point to the cube.

### Stage five: working in the cube

**Slide one, the sentence is a path.** Turn the cube to the side face, the
language view. A sentence runs across it, for example: module take cards from
the third participant's domain, into journal, publish.

A sentence is a path through the points, one point for each word. On the
language face it reads as a straight line of words. Turn the cube, and the
same path zigzags across origins and depths, because each word may be
answered by a point from a different participant at a different version. A
word can also reach out of this cube, to a point in another participant's
public cube, served from their own hosts. That path is the call, crossing the
cube, and across cubes, to run modules wherever they live.

Keep the words and choose different points, and the same sentence runs
through different automatons. Press run and each word lights up as its point
runs, and the results travel back along the path. Before anything runs, the
path shows which domains the sentence will touch, and every step that would
change something waits for your approval.

**Slide two, debugging in natural language.** A sentence fails. On the
language face, the break shows on one word's point, so you know which
implementation, from whose domain, at which version.

From here, the debugging is spoken. The viewer says module trials and sees
who else is already working on that word. They say module changes on the
nearest trial and read it, file by file. They open a trial subdomain of their
own domain, and say module take to bring in the piece they want to try. Each
sentence appears along the bottom as it is said, and each one draws its own
short path through the cube.

The trial is sealed, so nothing done there can disturb the live site or
anyone else's trial. Every other point the sentence uses stays exactly where
it was, because no version has been removed. The viewer steps the failing
point up or down its versions, halving the range each time, until the
sentence runs again. Other people can open their own trials on the same word
at the same time.

Everyone debugs against the same thing: the public cubes. A stranger debugging
your word sees exactly the points you published, at every version, served
from your own hosts. The failing sentence, its slice, and the sentences that
found the fault are just a set of signatures, so you can hand them to the
owner of that point and they replay exactly what you saw. When the fix is
promoted, it becomes a new point in the public cube, and everyone debugging
against it can now choose it.

**Slide three, flavours, plug and play, and the tapestry.** Zoom out to many
slices through one cube. Each slice is a different flavour of the same
program, and anyone can make a new one by moving a single point. The viewer
can shuffle through working slices; each one says how many points changed and
from whose domain.

Then the view pulls back out of the one cube, and all eight participants'
public cubes come into sight, each shown at its own root domain and served
from its owner's own hosts. Every thread lights up at once: wherever two cubes
hold the same point, a line joins them. These are the packages replicated
from one domain into another, the follows between domains, and the sentence
paths that cross from cube to cube. Starting from the root and running
outward, the threads weave the cubes into a tapestry of connected domains.
The cubes turn together to show their symmetry: every cube has the same shape
and is read the same way, and the same signature sits at the same word in
every cube that holds it.

The stage closes on the development process in one loop. You say what you
want in a sentence. It is drafted in a trial subdomain, debugged in sentences
against the public cubes, reviewed in public, and promoted with one pointer
move into your own public cube. The people who follow you get a badge, and
they replicate it whole or in pieces, on their own time, on files that never
move under them. Anyone can add a cube of their own, join it to the others by
taking a piece, and share in what everyone else has made. The closing line
comes from the repository itself: the hive is the sum of what we bring to
it.

## The tile tree

This is the draft tree. The names become the routes, so they should be agreed
before phase one.

The root tile is called hypercomb. It is the website artifact, and it plays
with the present word. Its first layer is the values layer:
simple, organized, collaborative, intelligent, accessible, and localized.
Documents and pieces relate to values by wearing marks, and one piece can
relate to several values. The planting script's tree holds this first layer
and nothing deeper yet; the stage tiles below are the draft of later layers,
which form the saved walk.

The first stage is one-server. It holds a server tile, with signed-index,
sig-named-files, and install-pointers under it; a host tile, with host-api
under it; a client tile, with core and opfs under it; and a request tile, with
verify-on-arrival under it.

The second stage is domains-and-subdomains. It holds a workbench tile, with
private-to-your-machine and register-for-publishing under it; a domain tile,
with claim, root-folder, root-shows-the-public-cube, and names-are-pointers under
it; a publish tile, with bytes-first and pointer-moves under it; a
trial-subdomain tile, with isolation, what-changed, and reviews under it; a
promote tile; a minify tile; a visitor-pin tile; and a stable-files tile, with
no-version-removed, clean-paths, and honest-limits under it.

The third stage is eight-participants. It holds a participants tile with
participant-1 through participant-8; a packages tile with core, cards,

tiles.

The fourth stage is the-cube. It holds points; replicate-and-create;

The fifth stage is the-sentence. It holds a sentence tile with one tile per

The transition bee also gets its own tile, so its code can be read and copied
like everything else.

The participants, domains, and packages are tiles in their own right. The
eight-participant strip, the cubes, and the sentence paths all draw from those
same tiles rather than keeping their own copies.

## The phases

The work starts small and grows. Each phase leaves something usable.

**Phase one: get the tiles into the hive.** Create the whole tree, with names
only and empty pages. That includes the stages, the shared objects, and
the transition bee's tile. At the end of this phase the presentation already
exists as hexagons anyone can walk.

**Phase two: lay down the work.** On each tile, leave a note saying what it
must show, which document it explains, and which code it points to. The to-do
list then lives on the tiles themselves, so anyone, a person or a model, can
pick up a piece.

**Phase three: build each object.** Each tile gets its own page, and each
page works on its own. A page starts whole; it is broken into web parts only
when it actually needs a second presentation, never in advance, and then by
adding a trait word at the layout layer and providing a new primitive atom
that has that trait. The shared objects come first, the participant, the
domain, the package, and the revision, because several stages reuse them. The
trial bubble, the cube, the public cube at the root, and the sentence path are
objects too, so they can be worked on in parallel without waiting for each
other.

**Phase four: tie it together.** The stage pages arrange the objects. The
links get their explore and next. The transition bee does the lift and the
reshaping, and the objects animate into place.

### One layer at a time, rebuilt whenever we like

The tree is built one layer at a time, always the first layer under a tile
that already exists, and each layer is looked at before the next one goes in.
The tile at the top is called hypercomb, and its first layer is the six
values, each with a short welcoming note.

Because nothing depends on anything, the arrangement is never final. Pieces
can be moved, regrouped, or rebuilt later, and every piece keeps working.
The documentation joins the same way: a document relates to the values and
pieces it explains by wearing marks, and that can be done gradually, one
piece at a time, without planning the whole structure in advance.

### The canonical loop: one layer, one revision, one publish

Every layer goes through the same loop, and it is the loop anyone would use.
First the layer's tiles are created and given their notes. Then the tiles are
given their pictures, by hand in the hive. Then the pass is
sealed as one build revision on the top tile, so every step of the work is
its own revision, and nothing is lost when the next step changes it. Then
the top tile is published to its own subdomain: its bytes go to the host and
the subdomain opens on the new head, so the layer can be seen. Publishing is
not joining or sharing a room; it only makes the bytes readable. The hive
asks for consent in the browser before anything is published. Then the next
step begins, and the loop runs again.

The first pass publishes the tiles only: hypercomb and its six values, with
their notes and pictures. Pages come in later passes, one layer at a time.

Before a publish, the package is checked. A published site runs the package
its publisher's install channel names, served from the host in its smaller,
minified form, so the newest revision of that package is published first.
A published site is read, never authored: features meant only for
participants, such as chatting with the assistant, are not loaded for
readers. Which features count as participant-only is the publisher's own
list, and the features word shows and changes it. Anyone who wants the AI
and every other feature opens the presentation in their own hive, where the
whole package runs.

### Simple first, then variations on every domain

The presentation is a live, virtual one that anybody in the world can use,
and it starts very simple. The first path is finished once: the hypercomb
tile and its six values, published at presentation.hypercomb.com, where
anybody can open it.

Then the variations begin, on our other domains. Each domain grows its own
small examples, where
automatons make new grammars and new sentences. The same sentence, run on
different domains, gives different results, because each domain answers its
words with its own implementations. Nothing needs to cross between domains
for this: the sentence is run on each domain's own hive, and the difference
is the point. This is the moment that brings people in, because they see how
much one grammar can do when every domain runs it its own way.

### Pictures for the first tiles

No picture made for any of the six values exists in the repository. The
illustrations from the narrated video only look right inside the video's own
styling, so they do not work as tile pictures on their own. A tile with no
picture of its own already shows a picture from the hive's default tile set.
A tile's pictures are offered by its name from the hosts you follow, and one
is chosen on the tile's images wall, so pictures for the values can be added
there, or supplied and offered under each value's name. No spoken word gives
a tile a picture from a file yet; that is a missing word, to be added if
pictures are to be set by sentence.

The tree, with each tile's note, lives in one file. A small
script plants one layer from it by speaking to the hive: it says go, then
create for each new tile, then files the note on each one. It sends no raw
operations. A dry run prints the sentences without saying them, and they can
also be typed by hand on the command line.

There is also an existing narrated video presentation of Hypercomb, built
from scenes with recorded narration. Its scenes and narration are material
this presentation can draw on, and its own tile can sit in the tree like any
other piece.

### Working in concert: an agent pool for every tile

The whole mesh is planted at once, and every tile's note is its task brief:
what it must show, and the documents and code it draws on. Then every tile
is given an agent pool of its own, so many agents can work at the same time,
each on its own tile.

**The agents.** An agent works in its tile's own conversation, under a
harness: the hive's agent loop, kept as a signed record that can be swapped
like a theme. Each tile's pool names the harness it runs under and the
models it may use. The models are reached two ways, both from localhost:
through OpenRouter, paid by its key, or through the machine's own
subscriptions, such as the desktop coding harnesses, by way of the local
bridge. Either way the agents speak the same sentences a person does, under
the same rules, and every change they make waits for a person to accept it.

A pool uses the minimum. The smallest, cheapest model that can do the job
does the everyday work. When a task needs more, a heavier model or more
thinking, the turn is handed up to a stronger model kept on the list for deep
work only, and it answers only when handed up to. And when the work keeps
asking for more than the pool has, that need is recorded as a request, so a
person can add the stronger model it asked for.

**The iteration.** Each agent gets fifteen minutes to do the simplest job that
gets its tile done, then five minutes to wrap up and make sure it works.
Every iteration ends the same way: the work is sealed as a build revision,
and the revision is published to the domains in the mesh.

**Versions across the mesh.** Because each iteration is published, different
domains end up running the application at different versions of the same
mesh. People can open them side by side and watch the application run across
its versions: the cube, live, with real domains along one direction and real
revisions along another.

**The exercise.** All of this runs inside Hypercomb's own harness, so the
presentation is also an exercise of that harness. Each iteration leaves a
record: which tile, which agent and model, what changed, whether it worked,
and the revision it produced. Reading those records shows how effective the
harness is, tile by tile.

### Who does what

This follows the pattern the proposal sites already use: a tree file, the
pages, a local preview, and a stamp step that writes the tiles and pages into
the hive. A cloud session can write the tree, the notes, and the pages, and
check them in a headless browser. The stamp runs on the owner's machine,
because the hive and its bridge are not reachable from the cloud. After that,
the tiles are edited in the hive like any others. The transition bee is real
module code and is built in phase four, once there are pages for it to hand
over to.

The presentation then goes through the very lifecycle it describes. It is
drafted on a trial subdomain, debugged in sentences, reviewed, and promoted
with one pointer move into its publisher's public cube, and its followers get
the update badge.

## Where the story and the documents differ

1. **Where the workbench is.** The workbench is hypercomb.io, or your own
   host's root domain, private to your machine. A trial subdomain is not the
   workbench: it is the public door where others try and review a change. It
   lives at try, dash, and the site's name, and promotion makes it the site
   itself. Earlier drafts of this plan called the trial the workbench; that
   was wrong.
2. **Which version is the truth.** For a visitor, the deployment is the
   minified package. But the documents treat the readable package as the
   truth, and the minified one as derived from it. The slides keep that link
   visible, because it is what keeps minified code checkable.
3. **Keeping every version.** Nothing is ever deleted, and old heads keep
   resolving. But a host only holds what was sent to it, and sharing a
   branch's full history is a per-branch choice. Versions last because they
   are replicated, which is exactly what the overlap in stage three shows. No
   single host keeps everything.
4. **Merging.** Two developers' changes are not merged into one. They exist
   side by side, and anyone can build on either. Taking a piece and laying it
   into your own build is the built way to combine work.
5. **What the root shows.** Today a domain's root shows its showcase. The
   public cube is a new view of the same published points, not a replacement
   for the showcase.

## Decisions

These are settled. Because nothing depends on anything, any of them can be
revisited later without breaking what was built.

1. **The primitive is a tile:** a word, its signature, a layer of children,
   and a meta layer.
2. **No separate primitive tile in the first layer.** The primitive is shown
   by opening the hypercomb tile itself, so the values layer keeps one
   relation. The sentence the viewer keeps, everything here is the same
   thing, used again, lives in hypercomb's meta layer.
3. **A layout's collapse:** the whole parent layout collapses to the layer it
   was defined on, along the overriding child's branch only.
4. **The hypercomb tile** is a label by default; the filter is shown as its
   other reading.
5. **Questions are typed as spoken words** joined by and, or, and not, which
   is how the command line already reads. Symbols can come later.
6. **The first layer is published at presentation.hypercomb.com.** Our other
   domains come later, each with its own variations.
7. **Sentences across cubes are labelled as where this is going**, because
   they are not built. Running the same sentence on each domain's own hive is
   built, and that is what the variations use.
8. **Tile names:** hypercomb, then the six values, as planted.
9. **The word direction** gives each word one position, with its different
   implementations as separate points at that position.
10. **Turning the cube** slides the points into the groups each face shows.
11. **The trait picker** offers origin, platform, and locale first; audience
    and reviews follow.
12. **The public cube is a choice beside the showcase** at the root, not a
    replacement for it.
13. **Heart and heartbeat.** The heart is a tile's current layer, and moving
    it is what makes the heartbeat. The two words belong together.

2. Whether a page's link can open a child tile from inside the slides view,

3. Whether a tile page's script can reach the hive's named effects, so parts
